The complete experimental process
Integrating and Evaluating a Fruit Fly Connectome Model for Visual Steering in a Driving Simulator
Yechuan Wei · 2026

The initial question: a useful directional response
The project started with low-speed simulated driving. The first question was smaller than full road following: could visual stimulation of an existing fly connectome model produce a measurable and repeatable directional response, and could that output then steer DonkeyCar? The upstream system already supplied a graph, sensory encoders, and action rules. I tested these parts separately so I could tell what the neural model did from what the upstream code's built-in fallback rules did. Unstable early responses led to tests of camera interfaces, learned readouts and internal parameters, followed by continuous driving and recovery evaluation. The timeline records each change of objective.

The upstream model and the added driving system
The starting point was a pinned version of fly-brain-minecraft. It supplied a MaleCNS-derived graph, cell identities and positions, a visual encoder, a spiking core, and a motor decoder. The retained graph has 176,422 nodes and 6,287,749 aggregated connections. It keeps neuron pairs with at least five synapses. I first separated this core from the Minecraft body and its fallback actions, then added headless probes and records. The camera and driving loop came later.

The first stimulus and output tests
The first model accumulated input and emitted spikes at a threshold. I compared silence, a known sugar pathway, brightness patterns, moving targets, and directly supplied visual features. I also removed or reversed the stimulus. Some feature inputs produced responses, while brightness alone gave weak steering responses. Activity could remain after the stimulus ended. The next step was an observable vehicle loop, followed by separate tests of visual input and learned output. The records do not give one common cause for every early zero response.

A continuous core with trainable connections
The fixed spiking core and its numerical output interface did not provide the internal gradient path needed here. C1 first explored a few global parameters. C2 then introduced continuous recurrent computation and a trainable gain on each existing connection. In the final controller, a gain can scale its signed normalized base weight from 0.25 to 1.75. The graph and connection signs stay fixed. Learning can now update allowed parameters from recorded experience. Driving quality still has to be measured in fixed-weight evaluation.

From an eight-by-eight image to 817 spatial samples
The assisted controller reduced each image to an 8×8 grid of 64 brightness values and added four route or vehicle hints. A learned 68-to-3,324 mapping with bias and tanh supplied 3,324 R1–R6 cells. C8 used that mapping with fixed weights. C10 instead converted the original 160×120 image to grayscale and sampled directions associated with the model’s eye columns. Of 1,769 directions, 817 were inside the camera’s field of view. Each sample is a continuous brightness value from 0 to 1. Centring, gain, bias and tanh turn it into a bounded input. Cells outside the view still take part in the graph, without direct camera drive.

Why L1 and L2 became the visual input
L1 and L2 cover all 817 visible sampling positions in the column annotations. The upstream inferred positions for R1 to R6 photoreceptors cover only 216 of them. L1 and L2 therefore gave the more complete engineering input map. There are 1,634 directly stimulated cells, with 817 of each type. Most samples go to one L1 and one L2 on the same side and column. Two annotation exceptions give one target at a left sample and three at a right sample. The display preserves those actual assignments.

Paired signal and no-input recurrent states
The final core uses signed continuous activity. Each internal update combines connected activity, bias, and current input, applies tanh, and mixes the result with the previous state. It repeats this calculation four times on the same image. The resulting numbers carry into the next driving decision. This is the recurrent state. Biases also produce activity without external input, so a second state receives no external drive. Both states share parameters and reset together. The readout compares their activity at the same moment.

Descending neurons and the steering readout
Descending neurons carry signals from the brain toward the ventral nerve cord, so the readout uses all 1,314 DNs in the graph. It subtracts the reference state, applies layer normalization, and passes the features through 64 hidden units to produce a steering distribution. Training samples from that distribution. Evaluation uses tanh of its mean. Steering ranges from minus one to plus one. Later scored driving learns steering only and sends a fixed throttle of 0.03. The replay colours show saved normalized DN features.

Collecting experience and updating with PPO
The collector stores the input used for a decision, the action, its probability under the collecting policy, the value estimate, and the reward and timing that follow. The learner reuses that batch while limiting how quickly the policy moves away from the collecting policy. This is the project implementation of proximal policy optimization, or PPO. Reward versions combine progress, offset, heading, and action changes. The timeline records those revisions. In camera-only operation, geometry supports rewards, reset placement, and scoring, rather than route hints to the policy.

What a checkpoint preserves
A checkpoint is a saved training snapshot. It can contain the core and readout weights, the optimizer’s running gradient statistics, and update counts. Restoring those statistics lets Adam continue from its previous learning state. I checked the file hash, saved stage and restored parameters rather than rely on filenames. Both final feedback conditions restored the same R23 checkpoint and optimizer state, then started new driving episodes and recurrent histories. The lineage follows those restoration records: a higher run number does not necessarily continue the preceding run’s weights.

Rebuilding recurrent history before a short update
After connection gains change, the old input history produces a different recurrent state. Before learning from a short sequence, the program replays saved brightness numbers with the current parameters and rebuilds both states. It computes the earlier prefix without gradients, then keeps gradients through the selected 16 decisions. This is truncated backpropagation through time, or TBPTT. The final gradient routing also separates the value prediction error from the policy update. Value loss trains only the value output. Policy and entropy terms train the shared readout and allowed core parameters.

Verified starts and accepted action intervals
Before scoring, the simulator reset the car, applied the brakes and checked the road and starting pose. Later comparisons used the saved road geometry and measured starts. Camera time, decision time and action-end time were recorded separately. A step that failed the timing or command-echo check stayed out of learning. The valid earlier steps and the rejected messages were kept. In fixed-weight tests, a failed attempt was kept and the whole case could be repeated after a verified reset.

Parallel simulators and one shared learner
I first ran complete model-and-simulator copies side by side. A 64-worker test partly failed; a revised 32-worker setup completed. Later, several cars shared one learner. The final setup used one L40 GPU, 32 CPU cores and 187,950 MiB of host memory for 24 training simulators, four development evaluators and four held-out evaluators. Each car kept its own recurrent state and input history. Evaluation data stayed out of learning. Roihu's x86 L40 nodes could run the Linux simulator and provide the compute for the large graph, paired states, four calculation rounds and repeated history reconstruction.

The final matched movement-feedback comparison
Similar images can occur while the car is moving or turning differently. The final experiment added previous accepted steering, speed and estimated turning rate through a new linear map to 3,324 R1–R6 cells. Its 9,972 weights fed tanh and then the recurrent core, without a direct steering-head shortcut. Both conditions used the same structure and started the new map at zero. One received movement values; the other received three zeros. The movement map learned nonzero weights while the zero-input map stayed zero. Existing connection gains, readout and exploration scale continued learning in both. R38 completed equal training and evaluation, with no clear overall improvement.

001. Start with a measurable model, before asking it to drive
Engineering check or repair

I separated the existing fly model from the Minecraft body so I could test its inputs and outputs before connecting it to a car.

Complete account
I began by separating an existing fruit-fly model from the Minecraft body that normally used it. The upstream project supplied the graph, signed connection weights, sensory encoders, spiking dynamics and an authored motor decoder. The retained graph contained 176,422 neurons and 6,287,749 aggregated connections. I added a command-line test entry point, controlled stimuli and recording tools; the vehicle adapter came later. 002. Check that silence and a known sensory pathway work
Engineering check or repair

Silence produced no spikes, while the known sugar pathway produced activity. This gave the later visual tests a working reference.

Complete account
I first checked two known conditions, each lasting 600 milliseconds. With no input, the network produced no spikes. The sugar-sensory stimulus described by the upstream project produced 59,957 spikes, including activity in the two MN9 neurons. Their population rates, measured in 50-millisecond windows, ranged from 30 to 90 hertz. These controls established that the program could produce a measurable sensory-to-motor response under the chosen settings. When later visual tests produced no turn, I could therefore investigate the visual interface and readout rather than assume that the whole program had failed.


003. A bright spot reached the visual input but produced no turn
Scientific attempt

A bright spot reached the early visual cells but produced no turning output. I next tried movement before changing the model.

Complete account
The first visual test asked whether a bright spot on a dark background could produce a directional output. Each sequence started a fresh network. Within the sequence, state carried through baseline, centre, one side, centre, the other side and stimulus withdrawal. A mirrored sequence reversed the order, and each phase lasted 500 milliseconds. I neither trained the parameters nor supplied a desired action to the output cells. 004. Motion alone did not activate the turning output
Scientific attempt

Moving the target still produced no turn with brightness input alone. I then tested the upstream object-feature channels separately.

Complete account
After the static spot produced no turn, I presented a moving dark target on a bright background. The neural core, internal parameters and readout stayed unchanged, and the upstream object-feature channels stayed disabled. This tested whether the missing ingredient was visual movement rather than brightness at a fixed position. Both mirrored sequences again produced zero turning output despite activity in the early visual input. I then enabled the upstream higher-level object-feature channels in a separate test. Keeping those conditions separate allowed me to distinguish responses to retinal brightness from responses to explicitly supplied object features.


005. Object features produced activity without reliable left–right control
Scientific attempt

Object features produced a turning response, but its direction depended on stimulus order and history. I next started each side from a fresh network.

Complete account
Enabling the object-feature channels produced activity in the selected descending neurons and a nonzero turning output. It did not produce a reliable left–right distinction. In the original sequence, mean raw turning output near the end of each phase was −0.52 for the left target, −0.64 for the later right target and −0.62 after withdrawal. The mirrored sequence gave +0.14, +0.06 and zero. Both stimulus order and internal history differed between these sequences. In one condition, left DNa02 activity continued after the target disappeared, placing part of the persistence inside the neural output rather than only in decoder smoothing. I next tested each side from a fresh network to reduce carryover between stimuli.

010. The self-rotation cue reached the intended side but left a lasting bias
Scientific attempt

The self-rotation cue activated the intended HS side, but turning stayed asymmetric and sometimes persisted after withdrawal.

Complete account
I supplied an analytic self-rotation cue through the upstream HS input, with uniform brightness and no objects. This cue was provided directly; it was not calculated from a camera image. Three seeds each received +300 and −300 source degrees per second. Positive input activated left HS and negative input activated right HS, confirming the intended side of entry. Turning remained asymmetric. The positive-stimulus tail means were −0.08, −0.32 and −0.32; the negative-stimulus means were +0.02, −0.04 and −0.20. After negative stimulation ended, the tails remained at −0.50, −0.50 and −0.46. I next ran matched zero-cue controls.

013. Neural output persisted after external input was removed
Scientific attempt

Turning-related activity continued after all external drive was removed. I then tested whether opposite stimulation could reverse it.

Complete account
Earlier withdrawal tests had removed the self-rotation cue while leaving ordinary brightness input active. I compared that procedure with removal of all external drive. Three seeds received both conditions, giving six trials with matching records before the intervention. In the removal condition, I cleared external Poisson drive and injected current after encoding, but kept cell state, pending synaptic events and decoder state. 015. Centring the wheel did not restore the earlier heading
Engineering check or repair

Returning the wheels to neutral did not restore the car's previous heading. The controller therefore needed to distinguish wheel angle from turning and heading.

Complete account
With the neural model disconnected, I held throttle at 0.1 and applied steering −0.15 and +0.15, returning the wheels to neutral between pulses and braking at the end. Across 144 packets, the steering phases changed reported yaw by approximately −2.607 and +4.498 degrees. During the neutral-wheel interval, yaw changed by only +0.0125 and stayed near the heading already reached. Final speed fell to about 1.36 × 10⁻⁷. The pulses began at different speeds, so they did not measure a matched left–right gain. They did show why the adapter had to distinguish wheel command, turning rate and heading: centring the wheels did not undo the earlier turn.


016. Opposite input did not reliably reverse the output
Scientific attempt

The input switched sides correctly, but the readout did not reliably reverse. I tested weaker input to reduce the persistent bias.

Complete account
Three new seeds received both negative-then-positive and positive-then-negative stimulation, giving six sequences with continuous state and 500-millisecond phases. HS activity switched sides in every signed phase, so the input itself was not stuck on one side. The descending readout was less consistent: only two sequences had opposite nonzero tail signs, and no seed did so in both orders. I reconstructed raw turning output from the six descending-population terms to roughly 10⁻¹⁶ error, locating the bias before decoder smoothing. I next reduced only the HS input magnitude to test whether weaker drive would reduce persistence while preserving direction sensitivity.


017. Weaker input reduced lingering activity without solving steering
Scientific attempt

Weaker input made withdrawal quieter but did not establish dependable left–right control. I then connected the current core to the car for observation.

Complete account
I reduced the cue from ±300 to ±150 source degrees per second, keeping seeds, stimulus orders, core and decoder unchanged. The upstream nonlinear formula reduced the specified HS generator rate from 100 to about 66.7 hertz. Matched records were identical over the first 1,000 milliseconds. Five of six new trials had zero raw turning output in all ten final withdrawal windows, compared with two reference trials. However, sequences with opposite nonzero stimulus-tail signs decreased from two to one, and one order kept a strong negative bias. Weaker input quieted the tail without establishing dependable bidirectional control. Although the recorded recommendation was to inspect another implementation, I chose next to connect the current core to a car and observe the loop.


018. The first closed driving loop turned away from its target
Scientific attempt

Complete account
