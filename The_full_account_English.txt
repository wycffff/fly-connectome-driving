The initial question: a useful directional response
The project started with low-speed simulated driving. The first question was smaller than full road following: could visual stimulation of an existing fly connectome model produce a measurable and repeatable directional response, and could that output then steer DonkeyCar? The upstream system already supplied a graph, sensory encoders, and action rules. I therefore separated them for measurement, so neural output could be distinguished from engineered fallback actions. Unstable early responses led to tests of camera interfaces, learned readouts and internal parameters, followed by continuous driving and recovery evaluation. The timeline records each change of objective.

The upstream model and the added driving system
The starting point was a pinned version of fly-brain-minecraft. It supplied a MaleCNS-derived graph, cell identities and positions, a visual encoder, a spiking core, and a motor decoder. The retained graph has 176,422 nodes and 6,287,749 aggregated connections. It keeps neuron pairs with at least five synapses. I first separated this core from the Minecraft body and its fallback actions, then added headless probes and records. The camera and driving loop came later.

The first stimulus and output tests
The first model accumulated input and emitted spikes at a threshold. I compared silence, a known sugar pathway, brightness patterns, moving targets, and directly supplied visual features. I also removed or reversed the stimulus. Some feature inputs produced responses, while brightness alone gave weak steering responses. Activity could remain after the stimulus ended. The next step was an observable vehicle loop, followed by separate tests of visual input and learned output. The records do not give one common cause for every early zero response.

A continuous core with trainable connections
The fixed spiking core and its numerical output interface did not provide the internal gradient path needed here. C1 first explored a few global parameters. C2 then introduced continuous recurrent computation and a trainable gain on each existing connection. In the final controller, a gain can scale its signed normalized base weight from 0.25 to 1.75. The graph and connection signs stay fixed. Learning can now update allowed parameters from recorded experience. Driving quality still has to be measured in fixed-weight evaluation.

From an eight-by-eight image to 817 spatial samples
The assisted model compressed each image to 64 brightness values in an eight-by-eight grid, alongside four route or vehicle hints. A learned dense map with bias transformed all 68 values to 3,324 R1 to R6 inputs through tanh. C8 used this mapping with frozen weights. C10 replaced that image path. It converted the original 160-by-120 image to grayscale and sampled at directions associated with the model eye columns. Of 1,769 directions, 817 lie within the declared camera view. Each brightness value is a continuous number from zero to one. Centering, gain, bias, and tanh turn it into bounded input. Cells outside the view remain in the graph and receive no direct camera drive.

Why L1 and L2 became the visual input
L1 and L2 cover all 817 visible sampling positions in the column annotations. The upstream inferred positions for R1 to R6 photoreceptors cover only 216 of them. L1 and L2 therefore gave the more complete engineering input map. There are 1,634 directly stimulated cells, with 817 of each type. Most samples go to one L1 and one L2 on the same side and column. Two annotation exceptions give one target at a left sample and three at a right sample. The display preserves those actual assignments.

Paired signal and no-input recurrent states
The final core uses signed continuous activity. Each internal update combines connected activity, bias, and current input, applies tanh, and mixes the result with the previous state. It repeats this calculation four times on the same image. The resulting numbers carry into the next driving decision. This is the recurrent state. Biases also produce activity without external input, so a second state receives no external drive. Both states share parameters and reset together. The readout compares their activity at the same moment.

Descending neurons and the steering readout
Descending neurons carry signals from the brain toward the ventral nerve cord, so the readout uses all 1,314 DNs in the graph. It subtracts the reference state, applies layer normalization, and passes the features through 64 hidden units to produce a steering distribution. Training samples from that distribution. Evaluation uses tanh of its mean. Steering ranges from minus one to plus one. Later scored driving learns steering only and sends a fixed throttle of 0.03. The replay colours show saved normalized DN features.

Collecting experience and updating with PPO
The collector stores the input used for a decision, the action, its probability under the collecting policy, the value estimate, and the reward and timing that follow. The learner reuses that batch while limiting how quickly the policy moves away from the collecting policy. This is the project implementation of proximal policy optimization, or PPO. Reward versions combine progress, offset, heading, and action changes. The timeline records those revisions. In camera-only operation, geometry supports rewards, reset placement, and scoring, rather than route hints to the policy.

What a checkpoint preserves
A checkpoint is a traceable saved state. It can include network and readout parameters, Adam accumulators, and update counters. Continuation checks the hash, saved stage, and restored parameter groups, rather than the filename alone. Both final feedback conditions restore the same R23 checkpoint and complete Adam history, then begin new driving episodes with fresh recurrent histories. The lineage follows those actual restore records. A later engineering version number does not automatically inherit the preceding run weights.

Rebuilding recurrent history before a short update
After connection gains change, the old input history produces a different recurrent state. Before learning from a short sequence, the program replays saved brightness numbers with the current parameters and rebuilds both states. It computes the earlier prefix without gradients, then keeps gradients through the selected 16 decisions. This is truncated backpropagation through time, or TBPTT. The final gradient routing also separates the value prediction error from the policy update. Value loss trains only the value output. Policy and entropy terms train the shared readout and allowed core parameters.

Verified starts and accepted action intervals
Reset first brakes and places the car, then checks road identity and pose before scoring begins. Later comparisons use captured geometry and measured starts. A seed name alone does not establish identical roads. Camera time, decision time, and action endpoint time remain separate. An interval that fails timing or steering echo checks stays out of learning. Recovery preserves the accepted prefix and the rejected raw records. fixed-weight evaluation keeps the whole failed attempt and replaces the whole case through the verified reset procedure.

Parallel simulators and one shared learner
The project first measured independent complete workers, then tested a partially failed 64-worker configuration, corrected a 32-worker setup, and moved to a shared model. The final system used one L40, 32 CPU cores, and 187,950 MiB of host memory. It served 24 training collectors, four development evaluators, and four held-out evaluators. Cars retain separate recurrent states and histories. Evaluation data never enter optimization. The native Linux simulator required x86, so it used the Roihu L40 visualization nodes. The large graph, paired states, four updates, and repeated history replay account for much of the compute cost.

The final matched movement-feedback comparison
Similar images can occur while the car is turning in different ways. The final experiment mapped the previous accepted steering command, speed, and estimated yaw rate to 3,324 R1 to R6 cells. This new linear projection has 9,972 weights and passes through tanh before entering the recurrent core. Movement and zero conditions have the same structure, but the zero condition sets all three inputs to zero. Both new maps start at zero. The movement map becomes nonzero, while the zero map stays zero. Existing connection gains, readout, and exploration scale continue learning in both conditions. R38 completed both budgets and evaluations, with no clear overall improvement.

001. Start with a measurable model, before asking it to drive
The earliest recorded work starts before there is a trainable driving system. We first take an existing fruit-fly connectome implementation and make it measurable on its own. The upstream project supplies the filtered graph, signed connection weights, neural dynamics, sensory encoders, and an authored motor decoder. The retained graph has 176,422 neurons and 6,287,749 aggregated connections. Our first additions are a headless experiment entry point, controlled stimuli, a recorder, and later the vehicle adapter. We remove the need for a Minecraft body and its behavioral fallbacks. We can then inspect input, neural activity, and output separately. This first version uses a fixed Java spiking network, with a half-millisecond integration step and 50-millisecond recording windows. The trainable continuous core comes later. First, we need evidence that the implementation works.

002. Check that silence and a known sensory pathway work
If a visual trial produces no turn, we first need to know whether the program is functioning. We therefore run two 600-millisecond controls. The silent condition produces zero spikes. The documented sugar-sensory stimulus produces 59,957 spikes, including measurable activity in the two MN9 neurons. Their 50-millisecond population samples range from 30 to 90 hertz. This checks a known sensory-to-motor path under our chosen settings. It gives us a useful reference for the visual tests: a zero visual output can now be investigated as an input or readout problem, rather than automatically being treated as a program that never ran.

003. A bright spot reaches the visual input, but produces no turn
The first visual question is simple: can a bright spot on a dark background produce a directional readout? This is the closest test of the original light-stimulus idea. Each sequence starts a fresh network. Within a sequence, state continues through baseline, center, one side, center, the opposite side, and withdrawal. A mirrored sequence reverses the order, and every phase lasts 500 milliseconds. We do not inject a desired action into the output cells, and we do not train parameters. The records show that retinal and early visual populations receive input. However, both raw yaw and decoded yaw remain zero in every recorded window. The next choice is therefore to test a moving stimulus, before changing gains or the output mapping.

004. Motion alone still does not activate the turning readout
After the static spot produces no turn, we try a moving dark target on a bright background. The neural core, readout, and internal parameters stay fixed. The upstream object-feature channels remain disabled. We change motion first because it addresses a concrete possibility: perhaps the static trial did not supply the visual changes this interface needs. Both mirrored sequences still produce zero turning readout, although early visual input is engaged. We retain that negative result. The next test enables an existing higher-level object-feature input as a separate condition. This allows us to ask whether the level of sensory entry matters, while keeping retinal stimulation and analytic features clearly distinguished.

005. A higher-level input produces activity, but not reliable left–right control
We read selected descending neurons as candidate motor-related outputs. Enabling the object-feature channels finally produces nonzero descending activity and turning output. The question immediately becomes whether the response reliably distinguishes left from right. In the normal sequence, the raw-yaw tail means are −0.52 for the left target, −0.64 for the later right target, and −0.62 after withdrawal. The mirrored sequence gives +0.14, +0.06, and zero. These sequences differ in both central motion and internal history. In one condition, left DNa02 activity also remains after the target disappears, so decoder smoothing is not the whole explanation. The input can influence the model, but it does not yet provide reliable bidirectional control. We next test each side from a fresh network, reducing the carryover from earlier phases.

006. The first vehicle connection stops before any experiment
In parallel with the neural probes, we need a working connection to the course simulator. The first connection fails because the simulator process is not running. No driving action is sent, and there is no vehicle behavior to interpret. We retain this as an engineering failure before the experiment starts. The next action is to launch the existing verified CourseSim executable and check its menu, readiness messages, and telemetry.

007. The simulator sends data, but decimal commas break strict JSON
Once the simulator starts, data arrive, but its Windows locale writes decimal numbers with commas. Strict JSON parsing fails. We make a narrow compatibility repair: try standard JSON first, repair only the affected unquoted numeric tokens, and retain the original bytes, error, and changes. We do not change the operating-system locale or the course assets. Decoding coordinates is only one part of acceptance. We still need to observe scene selection and the readiness sequence before treating the vehicle interface as ready.

008. Fresh starts do not remove the inconsistent response
To reduce carryover from a preceding target, each unilateral pulse now starts a fresh network. Three technical seeds each receive one left and one right trial, giving six recordings. Every trial has 500 milliseconds of baseline, 500 milliseconds of stimulation, and 1,000 milliseconds of withdrawal. Left-stimulus tail means are +0.12, +0.08, and +0.06. Right-stimulus means are −0.04, −0.02, and zero. These signs differ from an earlier continuous sequence. One left trial also develops a persistent −0.63 withdrawal tail, while the other five finish at zero. Fresh initialization has therefore not produced consistent bidirectional, recoverable behavior. We keep these failures and next examine a different documented input: the upstream HS self-rotation channel.

009. Telemetry from an already loaded scene does not verify scene loading
A later connection reaches a simulator that is already inside a scene. Telemetry are available, but the intended menu-selection and loading sequence has not actually occurred. This attempt therefore does not pass the complete acceptance check. We keep the record instead of rewriting it as a successful scene load. We then restart the experiment-owned simulator at its menu and repeat the explicit scene request. This is a lifecycle check, separate from neural control or driving quality.

010. The self-rotation input reaches the correct side, but leaves a biased state
This condition uses the upstream analytic HS self-rotation input. It is neither a camera image nor rendered optical flow. With uniform brightness and no objects, we supply +300 or −300 source degrees per second. Three seeds receive both signs. Positive input activates left HS, and negative input activates right HS, so the intended input side is engaged. The turning response is still asymmetric. Positive-stimulus tail means are −0.08, −0.32, and −0.32; negative-stimulus means are +0.02, −0.04, and −0.20. After negative stimulation ends, the three trials retain tails of −0.50, −0.50, and −0.46. We next compare these with zero self-rotation under the same background and seeds.

011. The matched zero-input control stays at zero
The three matched controls keep the same brightness, seeds, and timing, but set the HS self-rotation cue to zero throughout. Their HS activity and tail raw yaw are zero. This narrows the observation: the persistent bias seen earlier is associated with the tested nonzero input. It is not simply the same output appearing in every condition. Its internal mechanism remains unresolved. The next diagnostic removes all external drive after stimulation while preserving the internal state, so we can test whether continuing background input is required.

012. The scene and stationary telemetry chain finally pass
After returning the simulator to its menu, we explicitly request Warren, wait for vehicle readiness, and send zero steering, zero throttle, and full brake. We record 390 telemetry packets with finite position and orientation. Reported time increases from 0.0351 to 19.4851 seconds, and final speed is approximately 9.27 × 10⁻⁸ in simulator units. Six short non-protocol lines remain separate. The connection, scene request, readiness, and stationary-data chain now work. Steering signs, repeatable resets, and neural driving still require their own measurements.

013. Persistent output survives verified removal of external drive
In the earlier withdrawal condition, only the self-rotation cue went to zero. The normal brightness background remained. We now ask whether that continuing external drive is required for the persistent output. Three seeds each receive normal withdrawal and verified external-drive removal, giving six trials. Their pre-intervention recordings match. In the removal condition, we clear external Poisson drive and injected current after encoding, while keeping cell state, pending synaptic events, and decoder state. During the last 500 milliseconds, left DNa02 still fires at 28 to 40 hertz, and raw yaw remains negative. Continuing external input is therefore not required for this observed 1,000-millisecond residual. The source of the internal persistence is still unresolved. We next preserve state and test whether an opposite stimulus can change the output.

014. A sibling-import error stops the action probe before commands
The first independent vehicle-response probe stops during a sibling-module import. The isolated Python runtime does not automatically add the script directory to its search path. We repair the entry point by adding that resolved directory explicitly, without changing the runtime configuration. No experiment is created and no vehicle command is sent in this failed attempt. Only after fresh-process import and host-guard checks pass do we run the actual bounded action sequence.

015. Wheel centering does not restore the earlier heading
This test leaves the neural network disconnected and measures the vehicle itself. We hold throttle at 0.1, apply steering −0.15 and +0.15, return the wheels to neutral between pulses, and finish with braking. Across 144 telemetry packets, the signed phases change reported yaw by approximately −2.607 and +4.498. During the intervening neutral-wheel phase, yaw changes by only +0.0125 and stays near the direction already reached. Final speed falls to about 1.36 × 10⁻⁷. The two pulses begin at different speeds, so they are not a matched left–right gain comparison. The practical lesson is that wheel angle, yaw rate, and heading are different quantities. A neural adapter must state which one its input and output represent.

016. An opposite input does not reliably reverse the readout
We next test opposite stimulation directly. Three new seeds each receive negative-then-positive and positive-then-negative sequences, giving six trials with continuous internal state and 500-millisecond phases. HS activity switches sides in all signed phases, so the recorded input side is not stuck. The descending readout remains inconsistent. Only two sequences have opposite nonzero stimulus-tail signs, and no seed does so in both orders. We can reconstruct the raw yaw from the six recorded descending-population terms to roughly 10⁻¹⁶ error, showing that the bias is already present before smoothing. The next comparison changes only HS input magnitude. The recorded hypothesis is that weaker drive might reduce persistent state while preserving directional distinction. We keep the current core, decoder, and sequences as the reference.

017. Weaker input quiets the tail, but does not solve directional control
We reduce the input from ±300 to ±150 source degrees per second, keeping the seeds, orders, core, and decoder unchanged. The upstream nonlinear formula changes the prescribed HS generator rate from 100 to about 66.7 hertz; it does not simply halve it. The matched audit confirms identical initial recordings over the first 1,000 milliseconds. Five of the six new trials have exactly zero raw yaw in all ten final withdrawal windows, compared with two reference trials. However, opposite nonzero stimulus-tail signs decrease from two sequences to one, and one order retains a strong negative bias. Weaker drive makes the output quieter without producing dependable directional control. The recorded recommendation is to inspect another implementation. The user then explicitly prioritizes coupling the current core to the car for observation.

018. The first real closed loop turns away from its target
The first coupled run sends real vehicle feedback into the neural model and sends its output back to the same car. At this stage, camera images are recorded only. The input is target heading minus measured heading, multiplied by 15 and clipped to ±150. We supply that desired correction through the upstream HS field that originally represents perceived self-rotation. Decoded neural yaw, scaled by 0.15, controls steering, while throttle stays at 0.1. Every complete frame links input telemetry, a neural window, the sent command, and later telemetry. We obtain 109 frames: ten braked warmup frames and 99 moving frames. However, 95 moving inputs are saturated at +150. Steering is negative, heading error increases from about ten to 14.43 degrees, and a collision ends the run. Braking is requested, but no later telemetry verifies stationarity. The chain works; its semantics and timing need diagnosis.

019. A restarted loop records no complete frames
One restarted coupled run stops while waiting for fresh vehicle telemetry. Its manifest records “No fresh vehicle telemetry before deadline,” zero complete frames, and a final brake request. The network or simulator scheduling cause remains unknown. This is an engineering stopping point, and it has no driving frames to display. We preserve it separately and then use a new source revision for a short five-second run to check whether complete recording has recovered.

020. A five-second follow-up restores complete recording
The short follow-up uses a new entry-point revision but retains the original fixed throttle, target-heading cue, and neural steering mapping. It produces 44 complete frames and 143 telemetry packets. The scene request and readiness sequence occur, and the manifest marks a completed exploratory run. This verifies that short-form recording is working again. We do not attach another run’s heading-error numbers to it or treat completion as a driving-performance result. The same approach is then observed on the second Windows host.

021. The second host also shows weak steering and increasing error
On the second Windows host, the controller still uses the engineered target-heading cue rather than camera-based control. There are 83 complete frames, including 73 moving frames, plus one final command without a completed frame. Only seven moving neural windows produce nonzero raw yaw, all at −0.4. Smoothing carries negative steering into 72 windows. Heading error rises from 9.992 to 14.464 degrees before a collision. The clocks are also different: 4.15 seconds of neural evolution correspond to about 16.84 seconds on the recorded wall-time axis. This recording shows that neural output reaches the vehicle, while also exposing weak amplitude, unfavorable direction, and asynchronous timing. Only 76 of 83 images were retrieved; missing frames remain missing. The next system version uses actual camera input and observes the full motor readout.

022. The first actual-camera run is active internally but does not drive
When actual camera input first enters the model, we convert each 160-by-120 RGB image into continuous brightness and sample it along the model’s eye-column directions. This view covers 335 of 1,769 columns, or 18.94 percent. Unobserved directions retain the encoder’s history; they are not fabricated as black pixels. Vehicle commands now come from neural turning, forward, backward, and stopping channels, without imposed forward throttle. Across 83 fifty-millisecond windows, the model produces 1,112,088 whole-network spikes. Yet all 21 raw and decoded motor channels remain zero. All 73 post-warmup command frames are zero, and position stays unchanged. The camera is connected: every window contains sampled brightness. The specific failure is that the chosen motor interface produces no driving signal. We next add intermediate visual-population measurements.

023. Intermediate recordings separate early activity from silent motor output
The second camera observation mainly adds measurements. We do not train the network or increase its gain. Across 84 windows, there are 1,125,568 whole-network spikes, but all 21 motor channels and all 74 post-warmup commands remain zero. The added records locate activity more precisely. Photoreceptor and L1, L2, and L3 groups are active. The 2,225 Mi1 spikes and 521 T4 spikes occur only in the first one or two windows. T5 and the selected LC/LPLC groups remain at zero. L3 is also asymmetric between sides. Many photoreceptor spikes belong to members associated with unsampled history columns, so the global count cannot all be attributed to the camera. Waiting longer in wall time does not train this fixed model. We therefore move to controlled sensory conditions with explicit neural durations and matched controls.

024. The long sensory screen stops with resource failures
The camera observations motivate a systematic screen of the supported sensory inputs. The original plan contains 31 conditions and three seeds, or 93 trials. Each fresh Java process runs 30 seconds of baseline, 60 seconds of stimulation, and 90 seconds of recovery in neural time. The longer exposure is intended to reveal slow responses and persistent states. In practice, only 42 trials complete. Forty-nine are marked failed, one stale entry still says running, and one never starts. After the host reboots, no experiment process remains. Thirty-one of the 49 failures have explicit Windows memory-commit errors; the available excerpts do not establish one cause for the other 18. We retain completed results and missing outcomes separately, add a resource-error queue stop, and do not restart the whole matrix.

025. Independent CPU queues complete the priority long-screen conditions
To cover priority conditions faster, we use four independent CPU queues on remote workstation, which has more available memory. Each neural trial still has one integration thread. The installed GPUs are not used by this Java backend. Twelve conditions receive the same three technical seeds, giving 36 trials with the original 30/60/90-second phases. All complete without recorded failures. During the tilt-scalar stimulus, the brake readout averages about 0.201 to 0.203, falling to roughly 0.006 during recovery. Leg and wing readouts can remain active at the end of the 90-second recovery. The tilt input is a shared bilateral scalar, not a signed posture angle. These observations identify responsive interfaces and long-lived states, motivating a narrower contrast, tilt-amplitude, and ground-contact screen.

026. A small CPU screen also remains incomplete
This historical batch matters because it actually ran. At that time, the user separately authorized six headless CPU sensory trials on the authoring laptop: constant-vision control and left and right small objects, each with two new seeds. There is no vehicle or training. Three trials finish validly. Two streams stop at 118.4 and 124.4 neural seconds, and one trial never starts. A later process check finds no Java or screen runner, while the interrupted trials’ error files are empty. Their cause remains unknown. We do not count them as zero responses or assign the same memory explanation without evidence. Different seeds and workloads also prevent a fair CPU-speed ranking. The incomplete batch ends, and the next focused comparison uses remote workstation.

027. A targeted short screen exposes asymmetry and readout saturation
Once the long screen identifies responsive inputs, we do not repeat every sensory modality. We focus on three questions: weak looming asymmetry, the plateau under strong stimulation, and the nonmonotonic tilt–brake relationship. Twelve conditions and three new seeds use 10 seconds of baseline, ten of stimulation, and 20 of recovery. All 36 trials complete in about 25 minutes. Weak looming is clearly asymmetric between sides. Strong flight-yaw output reaches a plateau. Tilt-dependent braking is nonmonotonic, with persistent or seed-dependent recovery. A later inspection locates the plateau in the decoder’s 50-hertz normalization of a single DNp03 neuron; underlying rates still vary. We therefore add lower contrasts and longer recovery, preserving the original readout alongside a fixed common-scale offline comparison.

028. Lower contrasts and longer recovery separate different output tails
This follow-up addresses specific gaps from the short screen. We add looming contrasts 0.05 and 0.20 on both sides, retain selected earlier conditions and tilt inputs, and extend recovery from 20 to 60 seconds. All 39 trials complete. The 27 repeated recordings have identical scientific prefixes over their common first 40 seconds. Different outputs behave differently: selected brake, wing, or yaw channels remain active at the 60-second endpoint. In contrast, among 18 looming trials with an active common-scale DNp03 signal, 13 have zero output throughout recovery and five have only one nonzero initial 50-millisecond window. This separates a particular steering readout’s release from persistent activity elsewhere. The next question is temporal: can the responsive path follow short, repeated, and reversed events?

029. The analytic flight-yaw path follows short and reversed pulses
A ten-second constant stimulus cannot show whether the model can follow brief events. We now use nine schedules and three new seeds: one-second or 250-millisecond pulses, repeated same-side pulses, and left–right reversals. Each trial starts fresh, while internal state continues through the events and gaps. All 27 trials complete, containing 36 pulses. Original raw flight yaw and the fixed common-scale DNp03 candidate have the declared sign in every recorded pulse window. The first block of three correctly signed windows falls within zero to 150 neural milliseconds. Three-zero release blocks begin immediately in 26 events and after 50 milliseconds in ten, with no later rebound in the observed gaps. Other brake and wing outputs can still persist. We next test whether actual retinal images access a similarly useful path.

030. An empty protocol object stops camera capture before configuration
Before deciding how much visual field is available, we try to capture actual views. The first attempt reaches an already loaded scene and stops on an exact empty JSON object. In 3.26 seconds, it sends no camera configuration and accepts no image. We make a narrow repair: retain exact empty objects as unclassified no-ops, while other missing-type objects remain errors. After offline checks, a new capture attempt begins. The failed attempt itself supplies no evidence about camera geometry.

031. Actual wider, side, and rear views are captured
The second capture completes eight requested configurations, with three 160-by-120 images each: front views at vertical fields of view 60, 100, and 120 degrees; both sides; rear cameras A and B; and a return to the front. Vehicle drift is tiny, while image content changes substantially with configuration. Rear A and B contain matching images. The last initial front image and first return image match byte for byte, although earlier front frames differ slightly. The vehicle body also occludes part of the rear view. We have actual evidence that the protocol delivers different views, but not an independent angular calibration. The next comparison uses verified front images and explicit projection assumptions, without stitching an invented panoramic input.

032. Retinal brightness and analytic features behave very differently
We now compare the input levels directly. The 99 trials include expanding, static, and contracting retinal-brightness patterns under full, current 335-column, and wider 817-column coverage, plus analytic features and matched quiet controls. Each event lasts two seconds, followed by 22 seconds of observation. All 60 expansion and nine static events have zero output on all 21 original raw motor channels. Contraction produces only sparse takeoff readout. After expansion is removed, 19 trials show brief motor-channel events. Retinal flight yaw remains zero throughout these trials. In contrast, all 12 analytic-feature trials produce event motor output. Simply widening coverage does not solve turning. We next inspect existing early visual time bins, separating stimulus presentation from brightness restoration before comparing real camera images with controls.

033. Restoring brightness explains why event averages can mislead
This step reads existing records rather than running the model again. In the previous four-second stimulus phase, the image event occupies only the first two seconds; brightness has already been restored in the next two. Averaging them together mixes different interventions. We therefore align 36 selected recordings to actual onset and restoration. Photoreceptor and L1 activity already change during expansion, while much of the Mi1 and T4 response appears just after brightness returns. For center expansion, the first 250 milliseconds after restoration contain about 2,147 to 2,215 Mi1 spikes and 527 to 570 T4 spikes across seeds. T5 remains zero in the exported bins. This refines the input-timing interpretation and supports the planned real-image versus mean-brightness comparison.

034. A real image changes visual activity beyond its mean brightness
The real-image comparison uses two front-view coverage masks. For each, we present neutral brightness, a held actual image, or a uniform field with the same sampled-column mean. Six conditions and three new seeds give 18 trials. There is no vehicle, self-rotation cue, or analytic object feature. All trials finish, and 108 record hashes and regenerated summaries are checked. The held images produce visual activity different from their mean-brightness controls. Original motor output remains sparse, mainly around presentation or removal. This confirms that image content beyond its mean reaches the recorded visual measurements, while the original motor interface still lacks sustained useful turning. We therefore move toward an explicit learned external action readout, after checking the vehicle’s action, timing, and reset contract.

035. Acceleration and braking work, but the first reset rule rejects a real return
A learning environment needs more than images: it must reliably start the next episode. The first action probe completes positive throttle, coasting, and braking. Speed rises from about 0.000394 to 0.210886, remains at 0.173192 after neutral throttle, and falls to roughly 3 × 10⁻⁷ after braking. The reset check then rejects a 0.30713-unit return because it requires at least a 0.5-unit jump. The reverse and steering groups do not run. Since the returned pose matches the earlier reset pose, we retain the failed check and replace its overly large jump rule with an observed-reference check using fresh low-speed telemetry.

036. The revised reset check confirms returns and completes action-sign measurements
The revised start rule checks an observable return rather than assuming a reset acknowledgement. The vehicle must first be displaced by at least 0.05 units, then return within 0.01 of the same-run reference with fresh low-speed telemetry and at most one degree of yaw error. The new invocation completes all three action groups and both inter-group resets in about 10.6 seconds. Negative throttle produces reverse displacement, followed by further reverse coasting. Negative steering changes yaw by approximately −0.491 degrees, while wheel centering does not restore heading. We can now test continuous joint actions alongside camera and neural recordings.

037. Continuous two-axis actions reveal a route-measurement problem
This pilot varies steering and forward throttle continuously while recording the camera, fixed-core output, and vehicle telemetry. Neural output is observed in shadow mode; the prescribed schedule drives the car and is not treated as expert labels. The positive-first and negative-first episodes complete 74 and 72 transitions. All phases finish, and low final speeds are observed. The average full receive-to-receive interval is about 209 milliseconds, or 4.8 decisions per second, while each neural window still represents 50 milliseconds. A new problem appears in the route fields: large CTE changes and node-index jumps do not have an obvious relationship to position changes. Before using them as rewards or teacher targets, we audit the actual simulator geometry and its measurement rules.

038. Export the actual route before building progress rewards
The source audit explains the scale mismatch. Telemetry position and speed are divided by eight, while exported nodes and CTE are not. CTE also uses the previous tracked infinite line, whereas the packet may already carry the next node index. We then query all 259 actual path nodes once while braking, obtaining an open 42.929-unit polyline. Reanalysis reconstructs the original CTE accurately, but an unchecked global nearest segment can jump four or six arc units after a tiny vehicle displacement. We therefore build local association around the previous valid arc position, constrained by elapsed time, reachable movement, and heading.
